Getting Started

From installing the app to a provisioned space, in about fifteen minutes. You need to be a Confluence product administrator.

1. Install

Install from the Atlassian Marketplace. Confluence shows the permissions the app asks for; each one is explained in Permissions and security.

Nothing else needs configuring. The app starts building its space inventory in the background as soon as it is installed, so the admin console has something to show you within a minute or two.

2. Look at what you already have

Confluence settings → Apps → Space Blueprints → Inventory.

Before you create anything, this is a useful screen on its own: every space on the site with its page count and last activity. You can assign owners and review dates here in bulk, which gives you a register of who is responsible for what — even for spaces that predate the app.

3. Capture your first blueprint

Blueprints → Capture blueprint.

Pick a space you would be happy to clone — a project space, a client space, a team space with the structure you keep rebuilding by hand.

The app inspects it and shows a fidelity report before you confirm: how many pages it contains, whether it holds whiteboards, databases, folders or blog posts, and therefore what a copy cannot carry across. Read it. It is the honest part of the product, and it is shown here rather than discovered later.

Then set:

  • Name and description — what a requester sees in the gallery.
  • Default owner — who is proposed as owner for spaces made from this blueprint.
  • Role for the owner — which space role the owner is assigned on the new space.
  • Review interval — how far ahead the review date is set, in days. The default is a year.
  • Naming policy — optional, see the next step.

Blueprints are capped at 500 pages. If your source space is larger, point the blueprint at a subtree.

4. Define a naming policy (optional, recommended)

Naming policies → New policy.

A policy is a pattern the space name must match, plus a worked example the requester sees while typing. For example, a pattern requiring client spaces to start CL- with an uppercase client code, and the example CL-ACME-2026.

The editor validates live against your own example, so you find out immediately if the pattern is wrong. Attach the policy to a blueprint from the blueprint editor.

5. Decide how requests get approved

Settings.

  • Manual approval (default): every request waits for an administrator.
  • Auto-approval rules: pick a blueprint and a group. Requests from that group, for that blueprint,

provision immediately. The audit trail records them as auto-approved.

Also here: who may request a space (everyone, or named groups), the weekly sweep schedule and batch size, and how long audit entries are kept.

6. Send yourself a test request

Open the Apps menu → Space Blueprints. This is what your team sees.

Pick your blueprint, type a name — watch it validate against the policy as you type — accept the suggested key, pick yourself as owner, write a purpose, and submit.

7. Approve it

Back in Settings → Apps → Space Blueprints → Approvals.

The request detail shows exactly what will be created: the blueprint, the name, the key, the owner and what will and will not be copied. Approve.

Provisioning runs in the background. You can watch it: create space → assign owner role → copy pages (with Confluence's own progress percentage) → apply labels → record. A small blueprint finishes in well under a minute; a large one takes a few minutes.

When it is done the request shows a link to the new space.

8. Tell your team

Share the Apps → Space Blueprints link. That is the whole onboarding for a requester: they see the blueprint gallery, the request form, and their own requests.

What happens from then on

  • Every new space request runs through the same loop.
  • Once a week the app sweeps the site and refreshes the inventory: owners, review dates, last activity,

and drift where a space's roles, labels or owner have moved away from its blueprint.

  • Diagnostics shows the last sweep, the rate-limit budget used and any recent failures.

If something goes wrong

Every error carries a code, and Troubleshooting lists them all with what to do. Provisioning failures can be retried from the step that failed — the app never starts over and never creates a second space.
